# What Have We Learned from Cancer Genomics

Cancer stems from DNA disruption that causes abnormal cell growth and division. When scientists research cancer, they examine the complete set of DNA instructions in a cell, called the genome, and use genomic sequencing to read those instructions and identify mutations that cause cancer. 

DNA has four chemical building blocks: A, T, C, and G. An SNP, or single nucleotide polymorphism, is a variation where one building block is in another building block's position. SNPs are not harmful or cancer-causing, but they do help identify disease risks and genetic differences associated with cancer risks.

With mutations, there are two main mutations to be noted: driver mutations and passenger mutations. Driver mutations provide an advantage in cancer development, helping altered cells grow and survive. Driver genes are genes whose alterations can provide this advantage. A typical tumor contains 2-8 driver-gene mutations. On the other hand, passenger mutations do not give that advantage. This means that when sequencing, not all mutations are equally important, and it is crucial to differentiate the two. 

There are two important types of driver genes to know: oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Usually, proto-oncogenes help regulate cell growth and division, but when certain alterations make these genes active, they become oncogenes that can promote cancer. Tumor suppressor genes act as a brake on cell growth, stopping it.
